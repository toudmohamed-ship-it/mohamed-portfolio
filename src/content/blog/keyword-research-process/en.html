Keyword research is the work of finding the exact words your future clients type into Google, and deciding which page on your site should answer each one. Done well, it tells you what to write, what to call your pages and which searches are worth chasing. Done badly, you rank for searches that never bring a client. This is the process I use for service businesses, step by step.

What is keyword research?

A keyword is a search: "family lawyer Casablanca", "how much does a technical SEO audit cost", "GA4 not tracking forms". Keyword research collects these searches, measures how often they happen and how hard they are to rank for, and groups them by what the searcher wants. The output is not a long spreadsheet. It is a short plan: which page targets which group of searches.

For a service business, the most important part is search intent, the reason behind the search. Volume comes second. A search with 50 visits a month from people ready to hire is worth more than one with 5,000 visits from students.

The four types of search intent

Step 1: List what you sell, in your clients' words

Start with your services, not with a tool. Write each one down, then rewrite it the way a client would say it on the phone. A firm may call it "contentious employment matters"; the client searches "lawyer for unfair dismissal". Read your emails, call notes and WhatsApp messages: the words clients use there are your first keyword list.

Add the places you serve if your clients search locally, and the problems your service solves ("website not showing on Google", "no calls from Google Maps").

Step 2: Start from your own Search Console data

If your site is already online, Google Search Console shows the searches you already appear for. Open Performance, then Queries, and sort by impressions. Two groups matter most:

  • Queries with many impressions and a position between 8 and 30. Google already connects your site to them; a better page can move them to the first page.
  • Queries that do not match what you sell. They show which pages target the wrong words.

This is where the Moroccan B2B case study started: Search Console showed the pages were aimed at the wrong keywords. After retargeting them, without creating new pages, clicks went from 32 to 74 in three months and the average position from 34.2 to 14.1.

Step 3: Expand the list with keyword tools

Now put your seed list into a keyword tool to find variations and measure them. Each tool has a role:

  • Google Keyword Planner (free with a Google Ads account): search volume ranges straight from Google, by country and language.
  • Semrush or Ahrefs: estimated volume, keyword difficulty, the pages that rank today and the keywords your competitors rank for.
  • Google itself: the autocomplete suggestions, "People also ask" and related searches at the bottom of the results page show how people phrase their questions.

Set every tool to the right country and language. Volumes for "SEO consultant" in the United States tell you nothing about "consultant SEO" in Morocco.

Step 4: Look at the results page before choosing

For each promising keyword, search it on Google and look at the first page. It tells you three things a tool cannot:

  1. The intent Google sees. If the top results are guides, a service page will struggle to rank there, and the other way round.
  2. The format that wins. Lists, comparison tables, local map results, videos.
  3. Who you compete with. Large directories and national brands are hard to beat; small sites with thin pages are a sign of an opening.

Step 5: Group keywords and give each group one page

Keywords that mean the same thing belong on the same page. "Local SEO for lawyers", "law firm local SEO" and "SEO for attorneys" are one group, so one page. Two pages chasing the same group compete with each other, and Google often ranks neither well. This is called keyword cannibalization.

Step 6: Prioritize

You cannot target everything at once. Score each group on three questions, and start with the groups that score well on all three:

  • Does someone searching this want what you sell?
  • Could your site realistically reach the first page within a few months, given who ranks today?
  • Is there enough search demand to matter? For a local service, a few dozen searches a month can be plenty.

Existing pages that already get impressions usually come first: improving them is faster than publishing new ones.

Step 7: Use the keywords on the page, then measure

Put the primary keyword in the page title, the H1 and the first paragraph, and the secondary keywords in subheadings where they read naturally. Write for the person, not the keyword count. Then check Search Console a few weeks later: the page should start to appear for its group of searches, and its click-through rate tells you whether the title and description convince people to click.

Common keyword research mistakes

  • Choosing keywords by volume alone. High volume often means mixed intent and strong competition.
  • Ignoring the language clients really use. Industry terms rarely match search terms.
  • Two pages for one keyword. They split the signals and both rank lower.
  • Forgetting local and language variants. In Morocco, the same client may search in French, Arabic or Darija.
  • Never checking the results page. The tool says "low difficulty"; the first page says "directories only".

Summary

List your services in your clients' words, start from the searches Search Console already shows, expand with Keyword Planner and Semrush or Ahrefs, check each results page, group keywords so each page owns one group, prioritize by intent and realistic ranking, and measure in Search Console. The result is a short keyword map that tells you exactly what each page is for.
